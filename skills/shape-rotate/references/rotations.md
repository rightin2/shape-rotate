# Rotation and representation catalog

Read this when choosing rotations for a solo pass or assigning different rotation sets to agents.

## Representations (starting shapes)

Each one makes a different kind of misfit visible. Choose the one that matches the friction.

| Representation | Best at revealing | Sketch |
|---|---|---|
| Argument tree | Unsupported claims, orphan evidence | `Claim -> reasons -> evidence` |
| Causal chain or loop | Missing links, hidden feedback | `A -> B -> C -> (back to A)` |
| 2x2 | A hidden second dimension, false either/or | two axes, four quadrants |
| Tension map | Forces pulling against each other | pieces on opposite poles with the pull between them |
| Layered stack | Mixed levels of abstraction | principle / strategy / tactic / example |
| Timeline | Order problems, before/after logic | past -> now -> next |
| Hub and spoke | What is really at the center | one node, everything else orbiting |
| Venn of needs | Where stakeholders overlap or clash | circles per stakeholder |
| Funnel | Where a reader or customer drops off | wide -> narrow stages |

Mermaid works well for trees, chains and hub and spoke. ASCII is fine for 2x2s and stacks.

## Rotations

### Re-center
Pick each candidate piece in turn and place it at the middle. Ask: what orbits it, what falls out of orbit, and does the story get simpler? The right center usually makes several pieces suddenly obviously related.

### Invert
Steelman the opposite conclusion. List what would have to be true for it to win. Any of those that are actually plausible are objections the structure must house. Sometimes the inversion is simply the better thesis.

### Re-order
Try three openings: the conclusion first, the reader's problem first, and the single most surprising piece of evidence first. The opening determines the shape of everything after it.

### Change the reader
Rebuild the shape for a hostile skeptic, a domain expert, a decision-maker with two minutes, and a smart 12-year-old. Pieces the skeptic would attack must be load-bearing or cut. Pieces the 12-year-old would not follow need a concrete example.

### Change scale
Zoom in: tell the whole thing through one concrete case, person or number. Zoom out: place it inside the ten-year system or the whole industry. One of the two often provides the missing spine.

### Change modality
Express the shape as a picture, a short story, a table, a formula (X happens when A times B exceeds C), or a single metaphor. If the metaphor holds across every piece, it is probably the spine.

### Subtract
Remove the weakest piece, then the most popular piece. What collapses tells you what is load-bearing; what gets stronger tells you what was clutter.

### Merge
Look for two pieces that are the same idea in different clothes, or a cause and its effect listed as separate points. Merging often turns seven loose points into three strong ones.

### Flip the frame
Change the genre of the question: a problem into an opportunity, a cost into an investment, a decision into an experiment, an argument into a story of how you changed your mind.

### Borrow a structure
Map the pieces onto a known structure: problem, agitation, solution; situation, complication, resolution; thesis, antithesis, synthesis; hero's journey; a court case with prosecution and defense. Note where the fit is forced; that is where the gap is.

## Assigning rotations to agents

Spread the lenses so agents do not duplicate each other. A good default for four agents:

| Agent | Starting representation | Rotations |
|---|---|---|
| A | Argument tree | Re-center, Subtract, Merge |
| B | Tension map | Invert, Change the reader, Flip the frame |
| C | Hub and spoke | Re-order, Change scale, Borrow a structure |
| D | Picture or metaphor (modality first) | Change modality, Re-center, Change the reader |

Adjust to the friction: a "no spine" problem benefits from more re-centering; a "readers do not buy it" problem benefits from more invert and change-the-reader.
